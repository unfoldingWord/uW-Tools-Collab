# Understanding the unfoldingWord Translation Ecosystem: Supporting Mother Tongue Translators

## Introduction

The unfoldingWord (uW) translation ecosystem supports Bible translation work worldwide. This document explains how the interconnected translation resources work together. These resources help Mother Tongue Translators produce accurate Scripture translations in their heart languages. The translations are also culturally appropriate.

This guide shows how the unfoldingWord ecosystem helps you. It addresses common translation challenges. It reduces dependency on external consultants. It gives immediate access to expert-level biblical scholarship through gateway languages.

### Key Definitions

**Gateway Languages**: Strategic languages (such as English, Spanish, French, Portuguese, Hindi) that serve as intermediary bridges between the original biblical languages (Hebrew, Greek, Aramaic) and target heart languages. Gateway languages are major languages that Mother Tongue Translators can understand and use as a foundation for translating Scripture into their native languages.

**Mother Tongue Translators (MTTs)**: Translators who are native speakers of the target language and are working to translate Scripture into their own heart language - the language they know best and speak most naturally.

**Heart Languages**: The native languages that Mother Tongue Translators speak most naturally and are translating Scripture into. These are the target languages for the final Bible translations that will serve local communities.

### Translation Challenges: A Real-World Scenario

Read this common translation scenario. It shows why the unfoldingWord resource ecosystem exists:

**The Challenge**: A team of Mother Tongue Translators in a remote region translates the book of Romans into their heart language. They use a popular commercial Bible translation in Spanish as their primary source. Spanish is their gateway language. They also use basic study helps.

**Problem 1 - Hidden Cultural Adaptations**: In Romans 1:1, their Spanish Bible translates Paul's description of himself as "siervo" (servant). This word feels natural in Spanish. But the original Greek word "δοῦλος" (doulos) means "slave". This is a stronger concept. It implies complete ownership and submission. The team does not know this. The Spanish translation adapted the word for cultural comfort. In the translators' culture, the concept of slavery can communicate Paul's meaning more accurately than the softer word "servant". Without access to the original language, the team misses this chance for a more precise translation.

**Problem 2 - Metaphor Translation Barriers**: At Romans 3:25, the team finds the phrase "propitiation through his blood." This theological metaphor combines ancient Jewish sacrificial concepts with legal terminology. This terminology has no direct equivalent in their culture. The team spends days to understand what "propitiation" means. They also try to understand how blood relates to forgiveness in their worldview. Without cultural and linguistic guidance, they have difficulty to find clear expressions in their language.

**Problem 3 - Consultant Dependency**: These difficult passages accumulate. The translation team must then wait weeks, months, or years for a visiting consultant. The consultant helps them understand the meaning. The consultant also suggests translation approaches. These delays are frustrating. They make team members uncertain about their translation decisions. The consultant is helpful. But the consultant does not always understand their cultural context. The consultant can suggest solutions that do not work naturally in their language.

**Problem 4 - Inconsistent Terminology**: The team finds the word "covenant" throughout Romans. They translate it differently each time. They do not have a systematic understanding of how this theological concept appears across the entire Bible. Without terminology guidance, their translation lacks conceptual consistency. This consistency helps readers understand biblical themes.

**How the unfoldingWord Ecosystem Helps**: These resources work together. They address each of these common challenges:

- **Literal Translation (ULT/GLT)** provides a form-centric rendering that preserves the structure and wording of the original text, showing exactly what the Greek says about Paul being a "slave" rather than a softened "servant"
- **Simplified Translation (UST/GST)** demonstrates how to express complex concepts like "propitiation" in clear, natural language that can guide culturally appropriate translation approaches
- **Translation Notes** provide specific guidance for passages like Romans 3:25, explaining the cultural and theological background needed for accurate translation
- **Translation Words** articles give complete definitions of key terms like "covenant" and "propitiation," showing how they're used consistently throughout Scripture
- **Translation Academy** articles teach general principles for translating metaphors, cultural concepts, and theological terminology
- **Translation Questions** help the team verify that their translation effectively communicates the intended meaning to their community

The translation team does not wait for consultant visits. The team has immediate access to expert guidance. This guidance targets the original language words they translate. The team does not need to learn Greek or Hebrew. Three resources give them access to original language insights through their gateway language. These resources are the Literal Translation, the Simplified Translation, and the Translation Notes. The Literal Translation shows original structure. The Simplified Translation shows clear meaning. The Translation Notes explain context. The team can decide how closely to follow their gateway language source. They can also return to the original meaning. They can translate complex concepts with confidence and proven methodologies.

This scenario shows why disconnected resources create translation bottlenecks. An integrated ecosystem works differently. It lets Mother Tongue Translators work efficiently and accurately. They get expert-level understanding of the original text. They access this understanding through their gateway language.
